Module 1.1 Blog Post

Introduction

We need to be able to think holistically in order to abandon linear, fragmented thinking in presuming that the system is defined by the interconnections among components and its end goal. With this view, we can divide the world into the Five System Types proposed by Checkland namely Natural, Designed Physical, Designed Abstract, Human Activity, and Transcendental which can be successfully handled by the systems engineering technique.

Definitions

System

A system is a group of things that are working together as parts of a mechanism, interconnected network, principles, and procedures. In tech this goes a step further by allowing to accept input, process data, produce output, while also storing the data.

System Thinking

System thinking is the process of thinking of systems rather than a isolated part. That means considering a problem on a larger scale helping source a solution as a whole system rather than attempting to isolate the problem. This creates long-sustained success rather than small snapshots of success. Creating a understanding of a full image and always making sure that you start bottom up to determine the best solution for the entire system rather than specific sections.

Systems Engineering

Is the encompassing approach of designing, integrating, and managing complex systems over their lifecycles, guaranteeing stakeholder needs are satisfied based on the full scope of the work completed.

Shift to Holistic Thinking

In modern world technical environment, taking a project as a mere list of tasks is a formula to failure. We need to embrace Systems Thinking, a thinking process, which ceases to be linear but more holistic. System is not only the things that are included in it, but it is characterized by the Interconnections of elements in it, its Boundary and the purpose that it is finally intended to fulfill.

Explaining the Different Systems

Being able to breakdown how each of these systems play a larger part in how we create/ design change in the future in this digital environment, being organized and understanding how each play part is key to future literacy.

Using Checkalnd's Five System Types to categorize we discover these System types:

Natural Systems:

    • Natural systems are an open system with features and behaviors not controlled by humans or their actions. As they form spontaneously, without any human design, these systems tend to respond to the outside world on their own, without the human intervention. Examples include the human body, weather patterns, ecosystems, biological functions, and so on. However, a grasp of natural systems is important in the context of modern systems engineering as they often represent the environment in which engineered solutions have to function, with variables that are unpredictable and impose strict limitations on the overall system architecture or functionality.

Designed Physical Systems:

    • Designed physical systems are the physical real world expression of Human Design. These systems are made from physical components carefully designed to provide humans or automated agents with a very specific function to carry out. The examples of these systems are numerous, ranging from simple hand tools, such as hammers, to complex systems, such as cars, aircraft, computers and medical appliances. Our present digital world is built on designed physical systems that are embedded with electronics and are connected to physical interfaces, which enable the computational logic to engage with the material world.

Designed Abstract Systems:

    • Abstract systems are systems designed by a person with no physical existence, but constructed deliberately to assist the human mind to remain in structured and organized systems of thought. These systems have no physical correspondences and depend solely on a symbolic representation to explain or organize complex ideas and concepts. These range from philosophical ideas, mathematical theories, formal languages, and engineering models, among others. In a digital world, designed abstract systems are the backbone of digital information processing, organization and understanding, and they are realized as software algorithms, protocol architectures, data structures, and logic frameworks.

Human Activity Systems:

    • Human activity systems are intentional, individual humans-oriented systems that are deliberately designed by people to accomplish a shared goal or specific set of collective goals. There are certain characteristics of these systems which make them highly dynamic as they directly influence, demonstrate and control human behavior and interaction in the real world. Examples in everyday life include organizational hierarchy, politics, social groups and services to people. It is crucial in systems engineering, because the implementation of any digital transformation depends largely on the ability to match the behavioral processes of the people who are using the software tools.

Transcendental Systems:

    • Systems transcendental are the systems of absolute truth, metaphysics, divinity, and conscious nature. They work at the extreme range of human perception, and include unknown problems and complex universal properties which are not currently understood, let alone measurable, by science. They are not particularly relevant to systems engineering, but they provide an important edge to the future literacy and will bring designers back to the absolute limit of what is technologically possible today, and provide a touch of theoretical space that future discovery and innovation in the field will take place.

Complexity (necessary and accidental), breakdown in Communication, lack of Understanding, the evil traps that we have faced in complex projects, we will need a strict method on how to overcome these. Systems Engineering provides the common sense, multi-disciplinary approach necessary to overcome these challenges to deliver successful systems.

MBSE: The One Vocational Truth.

From the literature provided, it seems that Model Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) is a paradigm shift from the disconnected stack of papers, which is prone to maintenance errors, different interpretation from their human owners and has a significant lack of verifiability. Historically, projects have become so large that devoting all your time to reading and updating the same stack of paper folders wasn't always possible, and if a change was made in one dimension, you had to make it in other dimensions, which could mean that one was the wrong dimension, and you had no way of knowing. To address these weaknesses, MBSE is centered around intelligent digital models that can be linked together with requirements, architectures and interfaces all interwoven.

This modern methodology is centered on the digital modeling, the development of a digital model, which can be a Systems Architect Model (SAM) or textual system representations based on a language such as SysML V2, which is an authoritative Single Source of Truth. To enable early analysis, strict traceability and ongoing validation, as noted by Khushnood Adil Rafique et al., these central artifacts are shared amongst all stakeholders. MBSE not only brings this computation environment together in a centralized place, it also ensures that updates automatically flow through to ensure consistency within the system, enabling a multidisciplinary team to collaborate on the same reliable information throughout a product's life cycle.

Module 1.2 Blog Post

Defining the Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC)

The Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is more than simply a technical term. It is the backbone of how we take an idea for an information system and turn it into something real and usable, step by step, from the first trace of an idea all the way to the day-to-day work of keeping it running (Olipas, 2020). The SDLC has its roots in the early days of computer engineering, but its purpose remains the same: to give us a clear, disciplined path that helps us preserve quality, meet client needs, manage budgets, and keep risks in check (Laato et al., 2023). Instead of letting software development become a chaotic mess of code and guesswork, the SDLC gives us a structure—a game plan—that keeps everyone on track.

Every organization might tweak the SDLC to fit their own needs, but at its core, the process always follows a set of key phases that depend on each other (Hossain, 2023). It all starts with Planning and figuring out the scope—basically, asking if the project makes sense, if it fits the bigger picture, and what resources are available. Then comes the Analysis phase, where the team digs deep to figure out exactly what the system should do and what it needs to handle, from security to speed and everything in between (Hossain, 2023).

After that, the process moves into Design, where all those ideas and requirements get turned into real plans—blueprints for how the system will actually work, from the data to the networks and the way different parts connect (Gillenson, 2018). Then comes Implementation and Coding, where the engineers roll up their sleeves and build the system according to those plans. But the work doesn’t stop there. Next is Testing, where the team checks everything, making sure it all fits together and works as it should, catching any mistakes before the system goes live (Hossain, 2023). Finally, the system is deployed and enters Operations, where it’s put to use in the real world and maintained over time—fixing bugs, boosting performance, and keeping it secure (Saravanos, 2025).

Analysis of Core Life Cycle Models

When it comes to putting the SDLC into action, organizations have to choose the right model for the job. The choice depends on how complicated the project is, how much the requirements might change, and who is involved. Most of the time, you’ll see three main types: Linear, Iterative, and Incremental models.

Linear Models (e.g., Waterfall)

Linear models represent a highly sequential, plan-driven approach where development moves rigidly through predefined phases in a single, unidirectional cascade (Maidin, 2024). Under this procedure, a subsequent development phase cannot begin until all formal deliverables and milestone documentation of the preceding phase are fully executed, verified, and signed off by stakeholders (Tolido, n.d.).

Key Characteristics
  • Strict phase serialization
  • Rigid step progression
  • Intensive documentation obligations upfront
  • Absolute absence of overlapping workflows (Saravanos, 2025)
Advantages

Linear lifecycles provide an exceptionally high degree of predictability and administrative clarity. Because requirements are completely frozen before design or coding begins, project managers can easily compute precise timelines, form clear milestones, and maintain tight control over financial budgets (Hossain, 2023). It remains highly effective for large-scale, stable projects running inside highly regulated environments—such as aerospace, defense, or financial infrastructure—where engineering compliance and predictable delivery structures are critical (Saravanos, 2025).

Drawbacks

The primary vulnerability of linear models is extreme architectural inflexibility. Because it lacks feedback loops between non-adjacent stages, discovering an institutional requirement error or core architectural flaw late in the testing phase creates severe disruption, demanding costly re-engineering effort (Laato et al., 2023). It operates on the flawed assumption that clients can accurately articulate every technical requirement years before seeing a working artifact, frequently resulting in deployed systems that are technically compliant but operationally obsolete by delivery time (Tolido, n.d.).

Iterative Models

Iterative models discard rigid linear phase progression in favor of cyclic evolution. Instead of attempting to engineer a flawless system in a single pass, developers rapidly build a rudimentary, end-to-end version of the entire target system. This complete prototype is then subjected to repeated evaluation, refinement, and expansion over sequential, short development loops (Tolido, n.d.).

Key Characteristics
  • Cyclic workflows
  • Ongoing evolutionary refinement
  • Rapid prototyping
  • Strong reliance on short partner feedback loops (Laato et al., 2023)
Advantages

Iterative lifecycles provide high flexibility and risk mitigation when tackling highly volatile or poorly understood system requirements. By generating functional prototypes early in the project life, developers can safely reveal hidden design flaws, validate user experience expectations, and adjust software architectures flexibly based on real-world usage data (Tolido, n.d.).

Drawbacks

This constant state of systemic evolution makes project management inherently complex. Without rigorous scope boundaries and disciplined change-management frameworks, iterative projects frequently fall victim to severe scope creep, where continuous feature changes delay final system maturation, inflate baseline costs, and push delivery well past deadlines (Asher et al., 2024).

Incremental Models

Incremental lifecycles break down a massive system architecture into a series of smaller, standalone functional modules (Olipas, 2020). Each module undergoes its own independent mini-SDLC cycle—spanning analysis, design, code, and deployment. The system is then systematically delivered to the market or client in staggered, additive releases, with each increment layering new capabilities on top of a fully operational core platform (Hossain, 2023).

Key Characteristics
  • Staggered modular delivery
  • Functional decoupling
  • Parallel engineering tracks
  • Progressive value realization (Olipas, 2020)
Advantages

This model drastically reduces overall project deployment risk and accelerates time-to-market. Clients receive tangible, operational value through early increments without waiting for the entire multi-year infrastructure to finish development (Hossain, 2023). If an individual module encounters a severe failure, it remains safely isolated within that specific increment, preventing widespread system failure across the entire corporate infrastructure.

Drawbacks

The success of an incremental architecture places an immense cognitive burden on the initial system architects. The core foundational infrastructure must be designed to accommodate future, unbuilt components. If the basic interface standards are flawed, integrating later increments into the older core creates massive architectural misalignment, requiring expensive codebase refactoring (Gillenson, 2018).

The Importance of Life Cycle Thinking in Digital Systems

If we want our digital systems to last and keep working well, we have to consider more than getting them up and running. Life cycle thinking means looking at the whole journey of a system, not just the finish line. This mindset is what keeps our technology strong, efficient, and ready to grow as the world changes.

Systemic Design

Thinking in terms of the whole life cycle changes how we approach design. Instead of just reacting to problems as they come, we plan ahead, looking for challenges that might pop up down the road. Good engineers don’t just patch things together for today—they build systems with clean parts, strong ways to handle errors, and clear connections between data (Gillenson, 2018). This kind of planning keeps the code organized and easy for the next person to understand and improve.

Sustained Viability

Most people don’t realize that building a digital system is just the tip of the iceberg when it comes to cost. The real expenses pile up over years of keeping the system running (Gillenson, 2018). That’s why life cycle thinking matters. If we plan for maintenance, energy use, and future growth from the start, we can avoid the trap of technical debt—the hidden cost of taking shortcuts now that leads to bigger problems later (Maidin, 2024). Building with the long haul in mind keeps our systems affordable, secure, and strong, even as demands grow.

Systemic Evolution

Today’s digital world never stands still. Cloud platforms, operating systems, and user requirements are always changing (Laato et al., 2023). If a system can’t adapt, it gets left behind. That’s why life cycle thinking treats a system like a living thing, not a finished product. By building in flexibility and modular parts from the start, developers make sure the system can grow, take on new technology, and change its routines without needing a total overhaul (Maidin, 2024).

Module 2.1 Blog Post

Leveraging Mental Models and Jakob's Law

One of the key early decisions that could be made is the choice of interface metaphor. Other users are expected to spend the most time on other web pages and apps — this is Jakob's Law. Meaning, they come with preconceived notions and mental models on how a system should operate. As Jakob Nielsen, co-founder of the Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) said, a user's experience of other digital products sets the expectation to follow the consistency that was learned; otherwise the user's cognitive load is increased because he has to learn something new, which will only further frustrate the user (Nielsen, n.d.).

  • Supporting Usability: Standard conventions (e.g. search bar at the top; shopping cart at right top) make it easier for users to learn the interface that they will use. NN/g says this is an issue of internal and external consistency, the first being to maintain a single product or product family and the later being about adapting to those used already throughout the industry (Nielsen, n.d.).
  • Cognitive Efficiency: If a user's model matches with a design, they can use "System 1" (Automatic, Fast, and effortless) in order to navigate the design's functions.
  • Predictability: Patterns offer a sense of familiarity that helps users feel confident and highlight where to look for items to complete tasks. That's why the shopping-cart icon has become ubiquitous across nearly every ecommerce site: shoppers have already been given the shopping cart icon for the last couple years; it is already a mental model they've built with repeated exposures so they're staring brazenly expecting to see it in the top right corner (Supercharge Design, 2025).

The Power of Gestalt Principles in Information Hierarchy

From the beginning, when a person looks at text, their visual system organizes that text according to the Gestalt Principles. These are principles that are used to optimize our vision of our two dimensional world to see structures and aggregate objects.

  • Proximity: Objects that are grouped together are perceived as a group. Making connections early, based on grouping related controls or buttons together, greatly assists the brain in categorizing functions.
  • Similarity: Color, shape, size, is considered to perform a similar function. Parents will begin to understand what is happening to the buttons in a consistent manner. Early use of consistent button styles establishes functionality without the need for words.
  • Figure/Ground: This principle refers to the ability of the visual system to separate out the visual field into the figure (the object of interest) and into the background (the ground). In the absence of such a figure, the user's focus of attention will be dispersed by a design that is not clearly defined (e.g., low contrast overlays).

Prototyping as a Risk Management Tool

In the human-centered process, making ideas tangible through early prototyping is a mechanical necessity. We make because we believe in the power of tangibility and know that making an idea real reveals complexities that mere theory cannot.

  • Failing Early to Succeed Sooner: By making low fidelity prototypes, using cardboard, paper, or simple digital tools, designers can "fail early and often".
  • Feedback Integration: Prototype sharing with the user at a soon as it is possible, changes in the design can be accommodated according to people's real behaviour instead of the designer's guess.
  • Economic Viability: It is less expensive to modify a navigation flow on a paper prototype, than to rewriting code after implementation.

Structural Sabotage: When Early Choices Hinder Usability

Where design choices early in the project go against human centered design, the design can go sour quite unintentionally. If designers cede to the "wild creativity" or the practical aspects of what machines can do over what the human brain can do, then the outcome is a system that seems "mad," rather than the real thing.

The Perils of Peripheral Vision Neglect

Many of the mistakes made at an early state involve putting into account the physical constraint of the human eyes. Human vision is exceptional in just a small central part of the visual field called the fovea, that expands to just 1% of the visual field.

  • The Peripheral Blind Spot: Peripheral vision sees details greatly, colors badly, and doesn't see words at all — it's really good at seeing movement and seeing in the dark.
  • Hinderance: If a critical "Success" or "Error" message is displayed in the far periphery (for instance at the bottom corner of a large monitor), then the user has a high probability of ignoring it - statically, given that they focus their fovea elsewhere.
  • Inattentional Blindness: Even the red and boldest message can create a problem if placed where a user cannot expect it to be when he/she relies on a mental model.

Discriminability and Accessibility Failures

Choices, like color schemes and font size, that are made in the early stages of an aesthetic problem can be more than stylistic: they can also carry information.

  • Color Blindness: With approximately 8% of males affected by some type of color vision deficiency vs less than 1% of females (National Eye Institute, n.d.), males are much more likely than females to be color blind. If an early design decision means that only icons conveys meaning over colors (i.e. only green (good) and red (bad) without icons for example), the system is unusable to a meaningful portion of the audience, and with only one X chromosome per person, men are significantly more likely than women to be affected by the underlying gene and only one altered copy is enough to cause this deficiency (National Eye Institute, n.d.).
  • Contrast and Light: Ambient light and screen quality vary wildly These can range from extreme to extreme. A low contrast design — examples of this are light gray text on a white background — could taste very cool in a studio, but would be difficult to read in sunlight and by people with low vision.
  • Redundancy: Where there are color coding with shape there is also text labelling, to make sure information is available for both user's and bio constraints.

Information Overload and the "Wall of Text"

In early-stage content strategy, the amount of information-based content can be determined. A designer who neglects to consider "chunking" is unstaking his allegiance to the hard limits of human short-term memory. In his 1956 publication, "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two," psychologist George Miller determined that humans aren't able to hold more than about seven meaningful units, or "chunks," of information in working memory at a time, and that chunking these bits of information is part of the secret to achieving above the bump (Miller, 1956).

  • The Seven-Item Limit: Memory span for working memory is about 7 plus or minus 2 items. Sometimes referred to as Miller's law in the world of design, this finding is often used as a warning against applying the "magical number seven" as a rule to arbitrarily limit the number of items in a menu or navigation choices to that amount, but as a reminder to group items into smaller chunks that are summarized with clear labels to be understandable and memorable for end-users (Laws of UX, n.d.).
  • Cognitive Load: A "wall of text" is a very long text with no visual structure; it overloads the user's mind for scanning and processing. This frequently results in a sort of "TL;DR" effect in which users simply ignore the content.
  • Hinderance: If designs have no visual hierarchy, headings or bulleted lists, user has to coax information from the design, causing head fatigue and mistakes.

The "Silent Operator" Strategy: Making Design Invisible

The interface is now a "silent operator" in "highly" human oriented design. The objective is that without being made aware of the choices that were made in the design of the method, the user should be able to accomplish the goal of the method.

  • Subtle Guidance: If, at the correct moment, the user finds what he or she is looking for in a way that corresponds to Jakob's and Gestalt rules, his or her eyes, naturally, will focus on the right thing at the right time.
  • Embracing Ambiguity: The designers who can "waste a shocking amount of time without the answers" upfront are designers who are ready and willing to think in this invisible, intuitive way—the way that ultimately generates answers—who make it to these answers. They are flexible that the user can control the design process rather than imposing a technical framework on the user.
  • Desirability, Feasibility, Viability: Most successful products are ideas that are both desirable for people and technically feasible and financially viable for businesses. But the practice of HCD requires a first step with the humans—their hopes, fears and needs—in order to discover what is most desirable first.
