Designing for the Mind: Why Digital Usability Is a Battle Against Cognitive Load

Whether we are using a software program, application, or web path, we spend hours every day using these. Where a digital system is successful, it's like the bare minimum in terms of noticeable presence. We take lightly tasks and book flights and send messages, buy groceries and more, with very little effort. However, if an interface isn't well designed, we are felt a sharp taste of friction and frustration, from the moment we land on it.

The difference between a smooth and agonizing digital experience is one-size-fits-all. But it isn't about design, it's about function, about how much a system respects the weirdness, frailty and limitations of the human brain. Fundamentally usability is a measure of how well an interface correlates with our memory, habits and mental processes. If designers don't pay attention to these cognitive limitations, then the user is required to expend the mental effort in figuring out how to work with the screen.

How Memory and Mental Models Shape Interaction

To create anything useful, we need to learn about humans' processes of information processing and information storage. We use mental representations of the way things work in the worldmental modelsto a great extent in our brains (Norman, 2013). You don't have to begin with a blank sheet of paper when you build a mobile app. However, you can carry with you the whole of your life in the mechanics of things and other software interfaces. This is why you would expect a small magnifying glass to signify "search," a cart icon to mean "cart" and a gear icon to mean "settings."

As per one of the fundamental points Jeff Johnson (2014) puts forth, Designing with the Mind in Mind, this dependence on experience is a perfect fit. This dependence on the experience just so works out with one of the fundamental points Jeff Johnson (2014) discusses in his book, Designing with the Mind in Mind, "we use shortcuts and rely on habits." The brains of humans are energy conserving. Don't try to work out each and every pixel on a new screen or each word in what is shown on a new screen, we quickly scan through the layout for patterns that we recognize in our mind and are congruent with our prior mental models of the screen. When we "see" a system we think we have which is expected, our cognition does not dry out. When they don't meet those criteria we get stuck.

Moreover, human-made design space is very constrained by its working memory. Another of Johnson's (2014) point of evolutionary importance is that our perception can only process so much information, and our memory is faulty. Working memory is our "mental sketch pad" on which we temporarily store and process info. It has only a limited amount of information that it can store at a single momentit is estimated to store an average of seven items (van der Sloot, 2003), although recent cognitive research by the likes of Sweller (2011) suggests that this is nearer to just three or four chunks of information.

Changing screens as a result of software requests makes demands on our limited working memory (LSTM), such as asking a user to remember a credit card number on one screen and then tabbing to the other screen to manually key it in. Usable interfaces use the display as a place to store information - letting the user's mind do the rest.

A system needs to quickly match its behaviour with the user's patterns of thinking when integrating a new user into a new digital world, as modeled above. An absolutely unfamiliar mental model imposed on the user without anchoring or obvious metaphors leads to an immediate unwillingness to participate with the software, because it is cognitive fatiguing.

Reducing Friction Through Consistency and System Feedback

Human memory is not flawless and humans are very distractible, this makes the need for consistency of interface and IMMEDIATE system feedback very important, in order to rely heavily on these two main elements of user experience design.

The Power of Consistency

When using your application users don't need to forget how to use it again on every page that they have to visit. There should be no sudden, conversion magic from a "Save" button that is saute-hugging the bottom-right corner of one screen to the gray four bullet humongous round button "Save" in the top left corner of the next screen.

To build predictable environments, internal consistency should be used so that users are able to build automated habits. When the visual elements behave in predictable ways, users move their thoughts from slow and deliberate, and stressful, to fast and automatic (Budiu, 2014). So they do not see the tool, rather they see it through to the effect they are intending to get. This is emerging further with Jakob's Law of Internet User Experience that goes on to say that other systems that the user has used for most of their time will use are likely what that system should look like as well.

The Necessity of System Feedback

If you ever thought that you press the "Submit" button and nothing happened on the web page, and you pressed it four more times, in frustration, and you realized that you must have submitted duplicate "Submit" forms in your frenzy? It's textbook knowledge of the systems' feedback.

To ensure that human beings are immediately given feedback that their actions have been successfully registered in a system. Feedback is like the circle that completes the relationship of cause and effect. This might be a visual animation when the button is pressed down, it could be a loading spinner animation, a haptic "buzz" sound on a smartphone screen, or simply a nice success banner. Budiu states that navigator's anxiety is avoided and errors are greatly minimized because the system is constantly answering three basic questions during navigation, which is done by formulating clear feedback. Swiped on the iPhone, what happened to the system? What can I do next?

The True Cost of Poor Design: Cognitive Load in the Wild

When interfaces lack consistency, ignore mental models, or fail to provide system feedback, they create massive amounts of unnecessary cognitive load. Cognitive load theory states that since our working memory has a strict capacity limit, any mental energy spent fighting a confusing interface is energy stolen directly from the user's primary task (Sweller, 2011).

Educational literature distinguishes between different types of cognitive load, specifically intrinsic load (the inherent difficulty of the task itself, like calculating taxes) and extraneous load (mental effort caused by the poorly designed way the information is presented). Good design seeks to eliminate extraneous cognitive load entirely.

For example, a "classic," analog one that is very common is "Norman Door," which is a physical door fitted with a handle that looks like you should pull to open, but requires pushing open (Norman, 2013). The tactile functionality of the handle gives an illusory indication of how a handle is supposed to function, which stands in direct opposition to its actual use.

Notice the door handle illustrated above. The size of the handle, and its pleasing design, will encourage your hand to hold, and to yank. When the door really does need a pushit goes wrong! You muster up enough strength to stand there, stupefied and correct the mistake in your physical ability in that fleeting moment.

In the Digital Age we're constantly interacting with digital Norman Doors. Think about an old ecommerce site with a plugin filled out for registration. Imagine a user's form for the registration of an old ecommerce site. Only a password which is quite specialbased on non-standard security rules, (e.g., must include exactly two symbols, no two adjacent numbers, a set maximum password length etc.). Until you have completed the entire page, click "Submit" and then fail, though, the interface will not display the rules at all.

Worse yet, the page will regain the information you had entered the page (username, email and shipping address) when the page refreshes to display the error information. This design mistake requires you to continually utilize your working memory and must remember what previously went wrong as you re-type simple details. It is all a mental overload of no valueno value whatsoeverand just brought in because of an inwardly skeptical interface design.

Tesler's Law and the Balancing Act of Complexity

All application cases have a level of difficulty which simply is not going to be engineered out of it. There are certain things which seem simple and yet are actually very complex: tracking an international flight, managing a global supply chain, filing the income taxes. This is a fact that led to the so-called Tesler's Law, or the Law of Conservation of Complexity.

The rule was coined in the 1970s by computer scientist Larry Tesler at Xerox PARC; and the law says that any system has a level of complexity that cannot be reduced or removed (Saffer, 2007). The greatest question for designers of user experience is not what we have to do to remove a complex problem, but whom do we have to take care of a complex problem? Should we construct smart defaults and leave out the "smart shit-time compilation" ourselves, or should we let the software do the rest for us?

On the other hand, making one screen all kinds of information quite an intimidating place for a person to travel. The sweet spot is achieved when the underlaying software does the heavy lift suggestively, which is classed as "Simple Valuable."

Modern online checkout experiences are just a snapshot of shopper experience. In the earlier days of on-line commerce, checkout entailed filling out an extended, multi-web page form by typing all the details: credit card issuer, billing address, shipping address, zip code and banking details.

Nowadays, platforms use one-click purchasing systems. It saves the information securely ahead of time, or it may pull out the information about your city and state the instant you enter your zip code into the software, removing much of the hassle from the operation. The technical component, which is the comparison process of a secure financial transaction through international banking flowsis just as intricately structured as ever, but it can do this without involving the user; the user doesn't have to think about it.

Conclusion: Usability Is Respect for the Mind

Digital systems are useless than darn and useful only when they don't reject human nature, they embrace it. Clean interfaces, with consistent visual and behavioral associations to mental models we are surrounded by in our everyday lives; complete visual and behavioral consistency; immediate feedback from the system; hiding complexity behind the interface and so onall contribute to software that feels natural.

But usability is not an aesthetic accolade or an afterthought thrown in at the end of a software development cycle but an empathy that is so great it's empowering. It understands that your attention is finite and that you can't have as much as you're willing to give, and it implements systems to protect you from the loss of attention at every stage. With a mind on design, we unlock technology's potential to assist people to work, create, and connect, yet not become ensnared in the tools of technology.
